Issue 06  ·  The Bill Arrives

Tuesday, 29 September 2026

Week 40·Waning gibbous·Sun ↑ 6:57 ↓ 18:44 London

Britain feels better than it has in two years. The bills are catching up.

Twenty weeks have passed since the last issue of Cascade, and the world did not wait. A peace deal reopened the Strait of Hormuz in June and collapsed in July. Brent crude fell to $68.53 and climbed back to $130.80. Britain changed Prime Minister, had its driest July on record and brought in its worst wheat harvest in forty-five years. Through all of it, British consumer confidence rose, from −25 in April to −13 in September.

That gap is the story of this issue. The shock that hit our moods in the spring is now reaching our receipts: diesel at 198.3p a litre, mortgage rates at their highest since 2023, inflation heading for 4 per cent. Cascade is now monthly, with short notes in between when something big moves. This issue walks through twenty-three measures and says, for each, whether it is better or worse than in May.

    Chapter I · Work and wages

    Why does work matter?

    The cuts kept coming, but British jobs held.

    Tech firms have cut about 128,500 jobs so far this year, according to the Layoffs.fyi tracker, already more than in the whole of 2025. Meta began cutting 8,000 roles on 20 May and cancelled another 6,000 open positions to fund its artificial intelligence spending. Cisco, Intuit and Oracle followed, several of them naming AI as a reason. In Britain the damage has stayed narrow. Unemployment was 4.9 per cent in the three months to July, a touch below the 5.0 per cent of the spring, and the economy grew 0.4 per cent over the same three months.

    Pay is only just ahead of prices. Regular pay rose 0.6 per cent in real terms against CPIH, but the Bank of England expects inflation to reach about 4 per cent early next year, which would wipe that out. The October labour-market release will show whether the summer calm holds into autumn. The tech cuts matter less for their size than for their aim: they fall on the middle of the office, the jobs that used to be the safe ones.

      Chapter II · Housing and the cost of living

      How long can a mortgage hold?

      Borrowing costs are back at their autumn 2023 peak.

      The average five-year fixed mortgage reached 5.88 per cent on 18 September, according to Moneyfacts, the highest since October 2023. The Bank of England’s own measure, which runs a month behind, had risen to 5.25 per cent in August from 4.81 per cent in March. All six of the biggest lenders have repriced, some of them twice this month, after swap rates jumped on the renewed oil shock. Rents rose 3.8 per cent in the year to August, to an average of £1,400 a month, and the increase has quickened for two months running. Inflation rose to 3.1 per cent in August, with motor fuel the biggest push.

      The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75 per cent on 16 September, but three of its nine members voted to raise it to 4 per cent. The next decision is on 5 November. A rise would land first on households coming off cheaper fixes this winter. The one piece of relief is at the till: food inflation is 1.3 per cent, the lowest since 2021. Chapter V explains why that may not last.

        Chapter III · Health and care

        Are we waiting too long?

        The queue grew again over the summer.

        The NHS England waiting list stood at about 7.3 million treatments at the end of July, up from 7.22 million in February. 111,379 people had waited more than a year. Only 65.4 per cent were seen within eighteen weeks, against a target of 92 per cent, though that is 4.1 points better than a year earlier, and the list is slightly shorter than it was last July.

        There is no new life-expectancy reading. The Office for National Statistics publishes the next national life tables, covering 2023 to 2025, between December and January. Until then the last word stands: men 79.1 years, women 83.0, and healthy life expectancy for men at 60.7, well short of the state pension age. A resident doctors’ strike planned for June was called off.

          Chapter IV · Society and belonging

          Who are you willing to disagree with?

          Belfast burned in June. Most of the country stayed calm.

          In June, anti-immigrant rioting in Belfast saw homes and a bus set alight and police use water cannon. Anti-racism rallies drew thousands in response. No comparable unrest followed in Great Britain. Small-boat arrivals across the Channel are down sharply: about 16,300 people to 9 September, 47 per cent fewer than at the same point in 2025, according to Home Office figures analysed by the Migration Observatory.

          The deeper measure has not moved, because it is only taken once a year. In the 2026 Edelman Trust Barometer, seven in ten people hesitated to trust someone with different values, and Britain scored as more insular than the global average. The next reading arrives in January. One busy day can still change the mood: 781 people crossed on 24 September, the most of any day this year.

            Chapter V · Environment, food, supply

            Who pays for the next bushel?

            Britain’s worst wheat harvest in forty-five years.

            England and Wales had their driest July on record, after heatwaves in May, June and July. Provisional AHDB figures analysed by the Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit put the wheat harvest at 19.5 million tonnes, 6.8 tonnes a hectare, the lowest in forty-five years of data. August was the warmest August in NASA’s global record, 1.40°C above the 1951 to 1980 average. The UN’s food price index rose to 133.3, up 4.8 points since March, with sugar and cereals leading.

            A very strong El Niño is building in the Pacific. NOAA puts the chance that it becomes the strongest since 1950 at 75 per cent by the end of the year, and NASA’s forecast puts 2027 at 1.64°C above pre-industrial levels. British shoppers have not felt this yet: food inflation is at a five-year low. A poor home harvest, dearer world grain and costlier diesel for every lorry make that hard to sustain into winter. That last sentence is our judgement, not an official forecast.

              Chapter VI · Geopolitics and security

              How long is a strait?

              The strait opened in June and closed again in July.

              On 17 June the United States and Iran signed a fourteen-point memorandum that lifted the American naval blockade and reopened the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude spot fell as low as $68.53 on 2 July. The truce lasted three weeks. Iran attacked three commercial ships between 7 and 9 July, President Trump declared the ceasefire over, and American strikes resumed. On 20 September a single ship passed through the strait, against about eighty-five a day before the crisis.

              Brent spot peaked at $130.80 on 15 September, days after drones hit Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, and stood at $114.89 on 22 September. Diesel at British pumps reached 198.3p a litre on 25 September, on the brink of its all-time high. At the United Nations on 25 September Iran offered a seven-day plan to reopen the strait; the United States rejected it. Elsewhere, fighting in Ukraine grinds on and a June framework deal between Israel and Lebanon is fraying. The American midterm elections on 3 November are the next date that could change the calculation.

              Measures from this chapter

                Chapter VII · Governance and trust

                Who is in charge of the answer?

                Britain changed Prime Minister without an election, and the mood lifted a little.

                Keir Starmer stood down after election defeats, and Andy Burnham became Prime Minister unopposed on 20 July. Ipsos found 40 per cent of Britons viewed him favourably in late August, up eight points since July. The share saying the country is heading in the wrong direction fell to 59 per cent, from 64 per cent in June and 71 per cent in late 2025. YouGov’s latest voting intention, taken on 20 and 21 September, puts Labour on 23 per cent, with the Conservatives and Reform UK tied on 21.

                Trust itself is measured more slowly. The 2026 Edelman Trust Barometer put trust in government sixteen points lower than five years ago across the countries it covers, and 65 per cent worried that foreign actors inject falsehoods into national news. The next Barometer arrives in January. New leaders usually get a honeymoon. The November rate decision, landing on mortgages, will be the first real test of how long this one lasts.

                  Chapter VIII · Human outcomes

                  What does this all add up to?

                  Britain feels better than at any point in two years. America does not.

                  GfK’s consumer confidence index rose to −13 in September, from −25 in April, its highest since August 2024 and the first run of three monthly rises since the summer of 2024. People are more hopeful about their own finances and the wider economy, though they also said they were saving more and planning fewer big purchases. In the United States the University of Michigan index fell to a record low of 44.8 in May, recovered to 55.2 in July, and slipped back to 48.1 in September as inflation fears returned.

                  UNHCR counted 117.8 million people forcibly displaced at the end of 2025, the first fall in ten years, driven by people returning home. That figure predates most of the displacement caused by this year’s war with Iran and in Lebanon. British confidence has risen while fuel, rent and mortgages all got dearer. If food prices follow the harvest and the Bank raises rates in November, the October and November readings will show whether the recovery holds.

                    Appendix · The Watch List

                    Leading signals to keep an eye on

                    Five readings due before the next issue, and what each one will tell us.

                    1. World food prices, early October

                      The FAO index jumped 1.9 per cent in August. Another rise for September says heat and war are feeding through to the world’s grocery bill, and Britain’s low food inflation is living on borrowed time.

                    2. UK inflation for September, mid-October

                      The Bank expects inflation to climb towards 4 per cent. A reading above August’s 3.1 per cent keeps that path on track and a November rate rise in play.

                    3. Bank of England, 5 November

                      Three of nine members already voted for 4 per cent in September. Two more and Bank Rate rises for the first time since 2023, with mortgage offers moving within days.

                    4. American midterms, 3 November

                      Reports in Washington suggest renewed strikes on Iran are being weighed for after the vote. The strait, and every price downstream of it, hangs on what happens next.

                    5. El Niño, NOAA’s October update

                      A confirmed very strong event raises the odds of a record-warm 2027 and of further pressure on sugar and other crops grown across Asia.

                    Next month

                    Inflation, labour-market and consumer-confidence readings through October, and the FAO food index early in the month. While Brent spot sits above $110, the strait is shut in all but name.

                    Next quarter

                    The Bank of England decides on 5 November. America votes on 3 November. The ONS publishes life tables for 2023 to 2025 between December and January, the first word on whether the century-long rise has resumed. NOAA expects El Niño to peak between October and December.

                    Appendix · Small Graces

                    Things that improved since May

                    1. British consumer confidence has risen for three months running, the first such run since the summer of 2024.

                    2. Food inflation in Britain is 1.3 per cent, the lowest since 2021.

                    3. Small-boat crossings are down by nearly half on last year, meaning far fewer people attempting the most dangerous route into Britain.

                    4. UNHCR counted fewer forcibly displaced people at the end of 2025 than a year earlier, the first fall in a decade, because people were able to go home.

                    5. The share of NHS patients seen within eighteen weeks is 4.1 points better than a year ago.

                    Appendix · A poem

                    Notes from the summer

                    In June the ships came through, and we forgot to watch.

                    By July the fields were pale, the wheat stood short.

                    A new name on the door in Downing Street,

                    a lighter word or two across the kitchen table.

                    Then the tankers stopped again at the narrows,

                    and the board above the forecourt climbed a penny at a time.

                    We are better at hoping than we were in spring.

                    We are poorer at the pump.

                    The almanac writes both down,

                    the way a field remembers the year it was dry.
